\section{Counting operations}
%==============================================================================
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A first count: adding two vectors}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Let $\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\R^{n}$ and set $\vc{w}=\vc{u}+\vc{v}$. Here is a question
we have never asked before:

\begin{quote}
How many calculations must a machine perform in order to produce $\vc{w}$?
\end{quote}

\noindent Before answering, one condition. Addition of vectors --- like every
vector operation in this course --- is defined only when both inputs have the
\emph{same} dimension. There is no sum of a vector in $\R^{3}$ and a vector in
$\R^{5}$: the definition matches up first coordinate with first, second with
second, and would run out. So we may take both to have exactly $n$ coordinates.

Now count. Addition is componentwise, so
\[
  \vc{w} = [\,u_1+v_1,\ u_2+v_2,\ \dots,\ u_n+v_n\,] .
\]
Each slot of the answer requires exactly one addition of two real numbers, and
there are $n$ slots. Nothing else happens. So producing $\vc{w}$ costs
\[
  \boxed{\,n \text{ operations}\,}
\]
and the same count holds for $\vc{u}-\vc{v}$, which differs only in replacing
each addition by a subtraction.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What counts as an operation}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{defn}[Operation]
In a computational setting, an \textbf{operation} is a single calculation
carried out on scalar quantities by one operator --- one addition, one
subtraction, one multiplication, one division, one square root.
\end{defn}

\noindent Two pieces of vocabulary we will use constantly: a scalar addition is
an \textbf{add}, and a scalar multiplication is a \textbf{mult}. So the count
above reads: vector addition in $\R^{n}$ costs $n$ adds.

\begin{rmk}
Counting every operation as costing ``one'' is a simplification, and a
deliberate one. On real hardware a multiplication is somewhat slower than an
addition, a division is several times slower again, and a square root slower
still. We ignore that for now because the first thing worth knowing about a
computation is \emph{how the count grows with $n$}, and that pattern is the same
whichever weights you attach. We will revisit the assumption when it starts to
matter.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The dot product}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Now take $\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle$. Write out what the definition asks for:
\[
  \langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle
  = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n .
\]
Two distinct stages are visible. First, $n$ products $u_iv_i$ must be formed ---
that is $n$ mults. Then those $n$ numbers must be added into a single total.

How many adds does it take to total $n$ numbers? Not $n$. Adding is a
\emph{binary} operation, so each add consumes two numbers and returns one,
reducing the count of outstanding numbers by exactly one. Starting with $n$
numbers and finishing with $1$ therefore takes $n-1$ adds.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.95]
  \node[opbox] (p1) at (0,0)   {$p_1$};
  \node[opbox] (p2) at (2.6,0) {$p_1{+}p_2$};
  \node[opbox] (p3) at (6.0,0) {$p_1{+}p_2{+}p_3$};
  \node[opbox] (p4) at (10.4,0){$p_1{+}p_2{+}p_3{+}p_4$};
  \draw[opar] (p1) -- (p2) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{$+\,p_2$}
                           node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{add 1};
  \draw[opar] (p2) -- (p3) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{$+\,p_3$}
                           node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{add 2};
  \draw[opar] (p3) -- (p4) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{$+\,p_4$}
                           node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{add 3};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (5.2,-1.15)
        {$4$ numbers, $3$ adds --- in general $n$ numbers, $n-1$ adds};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent So the dot product runs in two stages:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.92]
  \node[opbox] (a) at (0,0)    {$\vc{u},\vc{v}$};
  \node[opbox] (b) at (5.6,0)  {$p_1,\dots,p_n$ \ \ ($p_i=u_iv_i$)};
  \node[opbox] (c) at (11.2,0) {$\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle$};
  \draw[opar] (a) -- (b) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{multiply}
                         node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{$n$ mults};
  \draw[opar] (b) -- (c) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{total them}
                         node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{$n-1$ adds};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent Adding the two stages,
\[
  \boxed{\,\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle \text{ costs } n + (n-1) = 2n-1
          \text{ operations}\,}
\]

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The norm}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Next, $\norm{\vc{u}}$. Written out term by term,
\[
  \norm{\vc{u}} = \bigl(u_1^{2} + u_2^{2} + \cdots + u_n^{2}\bigr)^{1/2} .
\]
Now three stages. Each $u_i^{2}$ is the product $u_i\cdot u_i$, so squaring all
$n$ coordinates is $n$ mults. Totalling those $n$ squares is $n-1$ adds, by the
counting argument of \S1.3. And finally one square root is taken, of a single
scalar --- one operation, no matter how large $n$ is, because by that point only
one number is left.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.92]
  \node[opbox] (a) at (0,0)    {$[\,u_1,\dots,u_n\,]$};
  \node[opbox] (b) at (5.0,0)  {$[\,u_1^{2},\dots,u_n^{2}\,]$};
  \node[opbox] (c) at (9.6,0)  {$\sum_i u_i^{2}$};
  \node[opbox] (d) at (12.8,0) {$\norm{\vc{u}}$};
  \draw[opar] (a) -- (b) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{square each}
                         node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{$n$ mults};
  \draw[opar] (b) -- (c) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{total them}
                         node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{$n-1$ adds};
  \draw[opar] (c) -- (d) node[midway,above,font=\scriptsize]{$\sqrt{\ \ }$}
                         node[midway,below,font=\scriptsize]{$1$ sqrt};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent Adding the three stages,
\[
  \boxed{\,\norm{\vc{u}} \text{ costs } n + (n-1) + 1 = 2n
          \text{ operations}\,}
\]

\begin{rmk}
It is worth noticing where the $1$ sits. The square root is applied \emph{once},
at the very end, to a scalar --- not once per coordinate. Miscounting this is
the most common error in a first operation count: the stages of a computation
operate on different numbers of quantities, and you must track how many are
still in play at each stage.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The projection}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Now something with several moving parts. Recall from the vector unit, \S12.4,
\[
  \proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}
  = \frac{\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle}{\norm{\vc{u}}}\cdot
    \frac{\vc{u}}{\norm{\vc{u}}} .
\]
The hard work is already done: we know what a dot product and a norm cost. What
remains is to take the formula apart and add up the pieces, being careful at
each step about whether the quantity involved is a scalar or a vector.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.4em,itemsep=4pt,
                  topsep=4pt]
  \item $\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle$ --- a dot product, so $2n-1$ operations.
  \item $\norm{\vc{u}}$ --- a norm, so $2n$ operations. The formula asks for it
        \emph{twice}, once in each factor, so as written that is $4n$.
  \item $\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle/\norm{\vc{u}}$ --- careful here. Both of
        these are \emph{scalars}, so this is a single scalar division:
        $1$ operation.
  \item $\vc{u}/\norm{\vc{u}}$ --- here $\vc{u}$ is a \emph{vector}, and
        dividing it by a scalar is componentwise. That is $n$ divisions, so $n$
        operations.
  \item Finally the scalar from step 3 multiplies the vector from step 4, again
        componentwise: $n$ mults, so $n$ operations.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Adding them up,
\[
  (2n-1) + 4n + 1 + n + n = 8n ,
\]
so the formula \emph{as written} costs $8n$ operations.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Doing it better}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Is there anything wrong with that count? Mathematically, no --- $8n$ is the
honest cost of evaluating the expression exactly as it appears. But to anyone
who has written code, the problem is glaring.

\subsubsection*{First saving: stop computing the same thing twice}
Why did we compute $\norm{\vc{u}}$ \emph{twice}? The two occurrences are the
same number. Compute it once, keep it, and use it in both places. That deletes
one of the two $2n$ charges:
\[
  (2n-1) + 2n + 1 + n + n = 6n .
\]
Nothing about the mathematics changed --- only the bookkeeping. This is the
single most common optimisation there is, and it has a name in programming:
\emph{common subexpression elimination}.

\subsubsection*{Second saving: do not take a square root you are going to square}
Look at what $\norm{\vc{u}}$ is used for. It appears twice, and both times it
divides --- so what the formula really uses is $\norm{\vc{u}}^{2}$. And by the
vector unit's \S11.6,
\[
  \norm{\vc{u}}^{2} = \langle\vc{u},\vc{u}\rangle ,
\]
which is a dot product: $2n-1$ operations, with \emph{no square root at all}.
Rewriting the projection in the equivalent form already given in \S12.4,
\[
  \proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}
  = \frac{\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle}{\norm{\vc{u}}^{2}}\,\vc{u} ,
\]
the count becomes
\[
  \underbrace{(2n-1)}_{\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle}
  + \underbrace{(2n-1)}_{\langle\vc{u},\vc{u}\rangle}
  + \underbrace{1}_{\text{one scalar division}}
  + \underbrace{n}_{\text{scale }\vc{u}}
  \;=\; 5n-1 .
\]

\noindent Notice the third saving hiding in that line. The old version made two
separate passes over $\vc{u}$ --- dividing by the norm, then multiplying by the
scalar --- costing $n+n$. Combining the two scalars into one \emph{before}
touching the vector reduces that to a single pass of $n$ mults.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Version} & \textbf{Cost} & \textbf{What changed} \\
\midrule
As written                    & $8n$    & --- \\
Reuse $\norm{\vc{u}}$          & $6n$    & compute the norm once, not twice \\
Use $\norm{\vc{u}}^{2}=\langle\vc{u},\vc{u}\rangle$
                              & $5n-1$  & no square root; one pass over $\vc{u}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent For $n=1000$ that is $8000$ operations against $4999$ --- the same
answer, at a little over half the cost. And the square root, which we noted in
\S1.2 is among the slowest operations available, has been removed entirely.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why count at all}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A fair question at this point is why any of this matters. The formulas are
correct either way, and a modern machine will evaluate all three versions
faster than you can notice.

The reason is that we are stepping out of mathematics and into computation.
Until now every object in this course has been exact and every operation free:
$\R^{n}$ is infinite, a real number carries infinitely many digits, and writing
$\norm{\vc{u}}$ costs nothing because writing is not computing. A machine has
none of that. It has finitely many digits per number, finitely many operations
per second, and finite memory, and every one of those limits will eventually
bind.

The computations here are small, and modern hardware and libraries are heavily
optimised for exactly them. But the habit is what matters: taking a formula
apart, asking what each stage costs, and noticing when the same quantity is
being computed twice. Do that on a projection and you save a square root. Do it
on an algorithm that runs a projection a million times inside a loop, and the
difference is the difference between a program that finishes and one that does
not.

\lookahead Two threads start here. First, we have been counting \emph{exactly},
producing answers like $5n-1$; usually the constants and the $-1$ do not matter
and only the growth rate does, which is what \emph{asymptotic} notation captures
--- the reason $5n-1$ and $8n$ are ``the same'' in a sense worth making precise.
Second, we have assumed a machine stores real numbers. It does not; it stores
\emph{floating-point} numbers, of which there are finitely many, and arithmetic
on them is not quite the arithmetic of $\R$. Both are next.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Exercises}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In each case, count the operations required, and keep the count \emph{as low as
possible} --- part of the exercise is noticing what the given information lets
you skip.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{E\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.8em,itemsep=7pt,
                  topsep=6pt]
  \item Let $\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\R^{200}$ be orthonormal. Count the operations
        needed to compute $\proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}$.
  \item Let $\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\R^{50}$ with $\cos(\vc{u},\vc{v})=1$. Count the
        operations needed to compute $\norm{\vc{u}-\vc{v}}$.
  \item Let $\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\R^{25}$ be nonzero and linearly dependent. Count
        the operations needed to compute $\cos(\vc{u},\vc{v})$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Answer key}

\noindent\textbf{E1.} \emph{Zero operations.} ``Orthonormal'' means both vectors
are unit length \emph{and} orthogonal, so $\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle=0$ is
given. Then
\[
  \proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}
  = \frac{\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle}{\norm{\vc{u}}^{2}}\,\vc{u}
  = \frac{0}{1}\,\vc{u} = \vc{0},
\]
and the answer is the zero vector, written down without arithmetic. Ignoring the
hypothesis and evaluating the optimised formula would have cost $5n-1=999$
operations to arrive at the same $\vc{0}$. The lesson is that recognising
structure beats computing.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{E2.} \emph{$3n=150$ operations, and the hypothesis does not
help.} Directly: $\vc{u}-\vc{v}$ costs $n=50$ subtractions, and taking the norm
of the result costs $2n=100$, for $3n=150$ in total.

It is worth seeing why the given information is no use. Since
$\cos(\vc{u},\vc{v})=1$ the two vectors point the same way, and expanding as in
the vector unit's \S13 gives
$\norm{\vc{u}-\vc{v}}^{2}
 =\norm{\vc{u}}^{2}-2\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle+\norm{\vc{v}}^{2}
 =\bigl(\norm{\vc{u}}-\norm{\vc{v}}\bigr)^{2}$,
so $\norm{\vc{u}-\vc{v}}=\bigl\lvert\norm{\vc{u}}-\norm{\vc{v}}\bigr\rvert$.
That is a genuine shortcut in appearance, but it costs two norms and a
subtraction: $2n+2n+1=4n+1=201$, which is \emph{more} than $150$. A shortcut is
only a shortcut if it is cheaper.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{E3.} \emph{$1$ operation.} Linearly dependent and nonzero means
parallel (vector unit, \S18.4), so $\vc{v}=\alpha\vc{u}$ for some
$\alpha\neq0$, and the angle between them is $0$ or $\pi$. Hence
$\cos(\vc{u},\vc{v})=\pm1$, with the sign being the sign of $\alpha$. To find
that sign, pick any index $i$ with $u_i\neq0$ and form $\alpha=v_i/u_i$: one
division. Then $\cos(\vc{u},\vc{v})=+1$ if $\alpha>0$ and $-1$ if $\alpha<0$.

Evaluating the definition instead would cost one dot product, two norms, one
mult and one division: $(2n-1)+2n+2n+1+1=6n+1=151$.

%==============================================================================
\section{How costs scale: asymptotic analysis}
%==============================================================================
Section~1 produced exact counts: $n$, $2n-1$, $2n$, $5n-1$. Exactness is
satisfying but it is rarely what we want to know. In computer science the
question is almost always how the count \emph{scales} --- what happens to the
cost as the input grows.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What an algorithm is}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{defn}[Algorithm, informally]
An \textbf{algorithm} is a finite sequence of steps aimed at solving a problem.
It usually has an \textbf{input} and always produces an \textbf{output}.
\end{defn}

\noindent Vector addition is about as simple an algorithm as exists. Its input
is the pair $\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\R^{n}$; its steps are the $n$ componentwise adds;
its output is the vector $\vc{w}=\vc{u}+\vc{v}$.

\begin{rmk}
Every algorithm emits an output --- an algorithm that produced nothing would
have solved nothing. An input, though, is optional: an algorithm that prints the
first thousand primes takes no input at all, and simply runs.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Operation count as a function of input size}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
To speak about scaling we need to say what is growing. For an algorithm on
vectors, the \textbf{input size} is the number of components, $n$. So write the
operation count as a function of it:
\[
  f(n) = n ,
\]
where $n$ is the number of components and $f(n)$ is the exact number of
operations vector addition performs. Note that $n$ ranges over the natural
numbers --- an input size is a count, and there is no vector in $\R^{2.7}$ ---
so the graph of $f$ is a set of isolated points, not a curve.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[xscale=0.82,yscale=0.72]
  \draw[axisline] (0,0) -- (9.0,0) node[right,font=\scriptsize,black]{$n$};
  \draw[axisline] (0,0) -- (0,10.4) node[above,font=\scriptsize,black]{operations};
  % ticks drawn top-to-bottom / right-to-left so the label lands clear of them
  \foreach \n in {2,4,6,8} { \draw[gray!70] (\n,0.16) -- (\n,-0.16)
        node[below=1pt,font=\scriptsize,gray]{$\n$}; }
  \foreach \y in {2,4,6,8,10} { \draw[gray!70] (0.13,\y) -- (-0.13,\y)
        node[left=1pt,font=\scriptsize,gray]{$\y$}; }
  \draw[RoyalBlue,dotted,thick] (1,1) -- (8,8);
  \foreach \n in {1,...,8} { \fill[RoyalBlue] (\n,\n) circle (2.4pt); }
  \node[right,font=\scriptsize,RoyalBlue] at (8.15,8) {$f(n)=n$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent The dotted line is interpolation, drawn only so the eye can see the
pattern; the algorithm exists only at the dots. That pattern --- the relative
geometry of $f$ --- is what we mean by the \textbf{scale} of the algorithm, and
here it is plainly \emph{linear}. The number of operations vector addition
performs scales linearly with the number of components.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The other algorithms}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We already have counts for three more. Writing each as a function of input size:
\[
  \underbrace{g(n)=2n}_{\norm{\vc{u}}},
  \qquad
  \underbrace{h(n)=2n-1}_{\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle},
  \qquad
  \underbrace{\ell(n)=5n-1}_{\proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}\ \text{computed efficiently}} .
\]

\begin{center}

\noindent What do the four have in common? Every one of them is a straight line.
The slopes differ and one is shifted down by $1$, but the \emph{shape} is
identical: all four algorithms scale linearly with the number of components.
Make the input as large as you like and that does not change --- a line stays a
line.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Big-O notation}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Since the shape is what matters and the constants are not, we want notation that
records the shape and discards the rest.

\begin{quote}
For a count that scales linearly in the long run we write $O(n)$, read
``big-oh of $n$'', and call it the algorithm's \textbf{runtime complexity} ---
also its \textbf{asymptotic runtime} or \textbf{time complexity}.
\end{quote}

\noindent So vector addition is $O(n)$. And so are the norm, the dot product,
and the projection, despite their different constants and shifts. Big-O is
concerned only with the underlying scale, which is set by the \textbf{dominant
term} of the count.

\begin{warn}[We are not measuring time]
``Time complexity'' is a misleading name, and it is worth being clear about it
early. We are counting \emph{operations}, not seconds. How long a program
actually takes depends on the processor, the language, the compiler, how the
data sits in memory, and what else the machine is doing --- two $O(n)$
implementations can easily differ by a factor of a hundred in wall-clock time.
What Big-O describes is how the operation count \emph{grows}, and that is a
property of the algorithm rather than of any machine that runs it.
\end{warn}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Finding the dominant term}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
For a polynomial count the dominant term is the highest-degree one. Suppose an
algorithm's count is modelled by
\[
  f(n) = 3n^{2} + 2n + 3 .
\]
As $n$ grows, $3n^{2}$ outpaces $2n$ and $3$ so completely that they stop
mattering, and the coefficient $3$ does not change the shape --- a parabola
stretched is still a parabola. So $f$ scales quadratically and we write
$f(n)$ is $O(n^{2})$.

When the terms are not both polynomials it can be less obvious which one wins,
and a table of values helps.

\begin{ex}[A table that nearly misleads]
Consider $f(n) = 2^{n} + n^{5}$.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r r r l@{}}
\toprule
$n$ & $2^{n}$ & $n^{5}$ & larger \\
\midrule
$1$  & $2$             & $1$          & $2^{n}$ \\
$2$  & $4$             & $32$         & $n^{5}$ \\
$5$  & $32$            & $3{,}125$    & $n^{5}$ \\
$10$ & $1{,}024$       & $100{,}000$  & $n^{5}$ \\
$15$ & $32{,}768$      & $759{,}375$  & $n^{5}$ \\
$20$ & $1{,}048{,}576$ & $3{,}200{,}000$ & $n^{5}$ \\
$22$ & $4{,}194{,}304$ & $5{,}153{,}632$ & $n^{5}$ \\
$23$ & $8{,}388{,}608$ & $6{,}436{,}343$ & $\mathbf{2^{n}}$ \\
$25$ & $33{,}554{,}432$ & $9{,}765{,}625$ & $2^{n}$ \\
$30$ & $1{,}073{,}741{,}824$ & $24{,}300{,}000$ & $2^{n}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Read only the first six rows and you would conclude that $n^{5}$
dominates. It does not. The two cross between $n=22$ and $n=23$, and past that
$2^{n}$ pulls away without limit --- by $n=30$ it is more than forty times
larger, and the gap keeps widening. So the dominant term is $2^{n}$, and
\[
  f(n) = 2^{n}+n^{5} \quad\text{is}\quad O(2^{n}) .
\]
\end{ex}

\begin{warn}
That example is the reason a table of values is a hint and not a proof. An
exponential eventually beats every polynomial, no matter how large the
polynomial's degree --- but ``eventually'' can be far to the right of where you
stopped looking. Big-O is a statement about the long run, so a handful of small
values can point the wrong way.
\end{warn}

\begin{rmk}[A tip: compare with a ratio]
A quick way to settle which of two positive functions dominates is to look at
their ratio as $n$ grows. If
\[
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = 0 ,
\]
then $f$ is negligible against $g$, so $g$ is the dominant one; if the limit is
$\infty$, then $f$ dominates; and if it is a positive constant, the two scale
alike. Two standard facts from Calculus~I settle most cases immediately:
exponentials outgrow all polynomials, and all polynomials outgrow logarithms.
For the example above, $n^{5}/2^{n}\to0$, confirming $2^{n}$ as dominant without
any table.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The formal definition}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything so far has been informal --- ``dominant term'', ``in the long run''.
Here is the precise statement.

\begin{defn}[Big-O]
For functions $f$ and $g$, we say $f(n)$ is $O\bigl(g(n)\bigr)$ if and only if
there exist constants $C>0$ and $n_0\ge0$ such that
\[
  \lvert f(n)\rvert \;\le\; C\,\bigl\lvert g(n)\bigr\rvert
  \qquad\text{for all } n\ge n_0 .
\]
\end{defn}

\noindent Every function we will use counts operations, so all of them are
positive, and the absolute values may be dropped:
\[
  f(n) \le C\,g(n) \qquad\text{for all } n\ge n_0 .
\]
Read the definition as licensing two concessions. The constant $C$ says we do
not care about a fixed multiplicative factor; the threshold $n_0$ says we do not
care what happens for small inputs. Proving $f$ is $O(g)$ therefore amounts to
\emph{exhibiting} a $C$ large enough and an $n_0$ far enough right that the
inequality holds from there on. You do not need the smallest such $C$ --- any
one that works will do.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Three proofs}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{ex}
Show that $f(n)=2n^{2}+2n$ is $O(n^{2})$.

For every $n\ge1$ we have $n\le n^{2}$, hence $2n\le 2n^{2}$, and so
\[
  f(n) = 2n^{2}+2n \;\le\; 2n^{2}+2n^{2} = 4n^{2} .
\]
Take $C=4$ and $n_0=1$. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\begin{ex}
Show that $f(n)=n^{4}+n^{3}$ is $O(n^{4})$.

For every $n\ge1$ we have $n^{3}\le n^{4}$, so
\[
  f(n) = n^{4}+n^{3} \;\le\; n^{4}+n^{4} = 2n^{4} .
\]
Take $C=2$ and $n_0=1$. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\noindent Both follow the same recipe: bound every lower-order term by the
dominant one, then collect. That recipe handles any polynomial.

\begin{ex}[Challenge]
Show that $1^{2}+2^{2}+3^{2}+\cdots+n^{2}$ is $O(n^{3})$.

Here there is no highest-degree term to bound against, because the number of
terms itself grows with $n$. The trick is to bound each term by the largest one.
Every index $k$ in the sum satisfies $k\le n$, hence $k^{2}\le n^{2}$; and there
are exactly $n$ terms. Therefore
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2}
  \;\le\; \underbrace{n^{2}+n^{2}+\cdots+n^{2}}_{n\ \text{terms}}
  \;=\; n\cdot n^{2} = n^{3} .
\]
Take $C=1$ and $n_0=1$. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A note on what Big-O does and does not say}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Complexity notation comes in several forms. Big-O is an \emph{upper} bound on
growth --- it says the cost is no worse than a certain shape. There are
companions for the other directions: $\Omega$ for a lower bound, and $\Theta$
when upper and lower bounds match. For this course Big-O is enough.

\begin{rmk}
Big-O is often described loosely as ``the worst case'', and for our purposes
nothing goes wrong in saying so. Strictly, though, two separate ideas are being
run together. \emph{Worst, best} and \emph{average case} concern which
\emph{input} you measure; Big-O concerns how a count \emph{grows} once you have
chosen. One can perfectly well speak of an average-case $O(n\log_2 n)$.

For the algorithms in \S1 the distinction happens not to arise: vector addition
performs exactly $n$ adds whatever the entries happen to be, and the same holds
for the dot product, the norm and the projection. Their cost depends only on
$n$, so best, average and worst case coincide. Algorithms where they differ ---
searching, sorting, elimination with pivoting --- are where the vocabulary earns
its keep.
\end{rmk}

\noindent Big-O is ubiquitous well beyond this course: it is the common language
of software engineering, numerical analysis and scientific computing, and it is
how the cost of every algorithm in the rest of this unit will be reported.

\lookahead One assumption remains unexamined. Throughout \S1 and \S2 we have
counted operations on \emph{real numbers}, as though a machine could hold one.
It cannot: there are infinitely many reals and only finitely many patterns of
bits. What a computer actually stores is a \emph{floating-point} number, and
arithmetic on those is not quite the arithmetic of $\R$ --- sums can depend on
the order you add them, and quantities that should cancel exactly do not. That
is the next topic, and the last of this lecture.

%==============================================================================
\section{The set a computer actually works in}
%==============================================================================
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Every subject starts with a set}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Mathematical subjects share a structural pattern, and it is worth naming before
we disturb it. In each one there is a \emph{set} of objects and a collection of
\emph{operations} or rules that act on them. Everything else --- the questions,
the observations, the conjectures, the theorems --- grows out of asking what
happens to those objects under those rules in one scenario after another.

Calculus is a clear case. The underlying set is the real numbers, together with
functions built from them. The operations on functions are governed by
properties of $\R$, and out of those properties comes the limit; out of the
limit comes the derivative; and out of the derivative comes the rest of the
subject. Nothing in that chain was available until the set was fixed.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Underlying set} & \textbf{Some operations} & \textbf{What grows from it} \\
\midrule
$\R$, and functions on it & limits, derivatives, integrals & calculus, real analysis \\
$\C$                      & complex arithmetic, conjugation & complex analysis \\
$\Z$                      & divisibility, congruence, counting & number theory, combinatorics \\
$\R^{n}$, later matrices  & addition, scaling, the dot product & linear algebra \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The underlying set plants the seed for a whole garden of mathematical
knowledge.

\begin{rmk}
The vector unit was exactly this pattern, carried out in front of you. We fixed
the set --- $\R^{2}$, later $\R^{n}$ --- then fixed the operations, and then
spent five parts asking what followed: when do two vectors span, when are they
independent, what does orthogonality buy, which subsets are closed. None of
those questions could even be \emph{stated} before the set and the operations
were on the table.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A computer works in a different set}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here is the disturbance. When a computer tackles a mathematical problem --- and
especially a \emph{continuous} one, a problem posed over the real numbers --- it
does not work in $\R$. It works in a peculiar set of its own.

\begin{defn}[Floating-point numbers, informally]
The \textbf{floating-point numbers}, written $\F$, are a \emph{finite} set of
numbers used to approximate the real numbers, each carrying only a fixed,
finite number of digits.
\end{defn}

\noindent Two consequences follow immediately, and both matter.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=4pt]
  \item $\F$ is \emph{finite}, while $\R$ is not merely infinite but
        uncountable. So $\F\subset\R$: every floating-point number is a real
        number, and almost no real number is a floating-point number. The
        machine is working inside a very sparse subset of the set the problem
        was posed in.
  \item The elements of $\F$ are \emph{not evenly spaced}. They crowd together
        near zero and spread apart as the magnitude grows, because a fixed
        number of digits buys finer resolution for small numbers than for large
        ones.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The picture below shows both facts at once. It uses a miniature
floating-point system --- three digits of precision in base $2$, with only four
exponents --- so that the whole set fits on the page; a real system has vastly
more points but is spaced in exactly this pattern.

\noindent Read the spacing across the picture: it \emph{doubles} at every power

Everything in $\R$ that is not a dot must be \emph{rounded} to the nearest dot
before the machine can hold it. That rounding is small, and for a single number
it is harmless. The question --- which is what makes this a topic rather than a
footnote --- is what happens when millions of such roundings accumulate through
an algorithm like the ones we counted in \S1.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Floats and doubles}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
You have almost certainly met this set already. Programming languages expose it
directly, and name the members by their \textbf{precision} --- the number of
digits each one carries:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Name} & \textbf{Storage} & \textbf{Roughly} \\
\midrule
\texttt{float}, single precision, \texttt{float32}
  & $32$ bits & about $7$ decimal digits \\
\texttt{double}, double precision, \texttt{float64}
  & $64$ bits & about $16$ decimal digits \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent A \texttt{double} is the default nearly everywhere it matters: it is
what Python's \texttt{float} is, what MATLAB uses unless told otherwise, and
what numerical libraries assume. A \texttt{float} halves the storage and the
precision, which is a real trade in graphics and machine learning and a
liability almost everywhere else.

The word \emph{precision} is the one to hold on to. It does not mean the numbers
are approximately right in some vague sense; it means each one carries a
specific, countable number of digits, and everything past that is gone.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Base 2}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One more ingredient before we can say what a floating-point number actually
\emph{is}. A classical computer stores everything --- numbers included --- as
patterns of $0$s and $1$s, which is to say in \textbf{binary}.

Binary is not exotic. It is simply a numerical \emph{base}, base $2$, and we
have been conditioned by a decade of schooling to think in base $10$ instead ---
for no better reason than that people have ten fingers. In any base $b$, a
string of digits is shorthand for a sum of multiples of powers of $b$:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Place values} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
$237_{10}$  & $2(10^{2}) + 3(10^{1}) + 7(10^{0})$
            & $200+30+7 = 237$ \\
$1011_{2}$  & $1(2^{3}) + 0(2^{2}) + 1(2^{1}) + 1(2^{0})$
            & $8+0+2+1 = 11$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The same idea continues to the right of the point, with negative
powers:
\[
  0.101_{2}
  = 1\bigl(2^{-1}\bigr) + 0\bigl(2^{-2}\bigr) + 1\bigl(2^{-3}\bigr)
  = \tfrac12 + \tfrac18
  = 0.625 .
\]
So the toy system in the figure above was nothing more than this: a few binary
digits, scaled by a power of two.

\lookahead A base is a choice of bookkeeping, and normally an innocent one ---
$0.625$ is $0.625$ whether you write it in base $10$ or as $0.101_{2}$. But the
choice is not always innocent, because a fraction that terminates in one base
need not terminate in another. That is where the surprises live, and it is where
we pick up next.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{From base 10 to base 2}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Reading a binary string is the easy direction: multiply out the place values, as
above. Producing one is the direction we need, and it takes an algorithm. Split
the number at the point and treat the two halves separately, because they obey
different rules.

\subsubsection*{The integer part: repeated division by 2}
Divide by $2$, record the remainder, replace the number by the quotient, and
repeat until the quotient is $0$. The remainders, read \emph{bottom to top}, are
the binary digits.

\needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{ex}
Convert $13_{10}$ to base $2$.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Division} & \textbf{Quotient} & \textbf{Remainder} \\
\midrule
$13 \div 2$ & $6$ & $1$ \quad$\leftarrow$ read last \\
$6  \div 2$ & $3$ & $0$ \\
$3  \div 2$ & $1$ & $1$ \\
$1  \div 2$ & $0$ & $1$ \quad$\leftarrow$ read first \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Reading upward, $13_{10}=1101_{2}$. Check: $8+4+0+1=13$.
\hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\noindent The algorithm is not a trick. The first remainder answers ``is this
number odd?'', which is exactly the $2^{0}$ digit; dividing by $2$ then shifts
the remaining digits one place right so that the next one can be asked the same
question.

\subsubsection*{The fractional part: repeated multiplication by 2}
Multiply by $2$. The integer part of the result --- necessarily $0$ or $1$ ---
is the next binary digit. Discard it, keep the fraction that remains, and
repeat. The digits, read \emph{top to bottom}, are the binary expansion. Stop
when the fraction reaches $0$, or when you have as many digits as you can
store.

\begin{ex}
Convert $0.6875_{10}$ to base $2$.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Product} & \textbf{Result} & \textbf{Digit} & \textbf{Carry forward} \\
\midrule
$0.6875\times2$ & $1.375$ & $1$ \quad$\leftarrow$ read first & $0.375$ \\
$0.375 \times2$ & $0.75$  & $0$ & $0.75$ \\
$0.75  \times2$ & $1.5$   & $1$ & $0.5$ \\
$0.5   \times2$ & $1.0$   & $1$ \quad$\leftarrow$ read last  & $0$ \ (stop) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Reading downward, $0.6875_{10}=0.1011_{2}$. Check:
$\tfrac12+\tfrac18+\tfrac1{16}=0.6875$. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\noindent Putting the halves back together,
\[
  13.6875_{10} \;=\; 1101.1011_{2},
\]
and we will encode exactly this number into hardware in \S3.8.

\subsubsection*{When the algorithm does not stop}
The integer algorithm always terminates --- the quotient strictly decreases and
must reach $0$. The fractional one need not.

\begin{ex}
Convert $0.1_{10}$ to base $2$.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Product} & \textbf{Result} & \textbf{Digit} & \textbf{Carry forward} \\
\midrule
$0.1\times2$ & $0.2$ & $0$ & $0.2$ \\
$0.2\times2$ & $0.4$ & $0$ & $0.4$ \\
$0.4\times2$ & $0.8$ & $0$ & $0.8$ \\
$0.8\times2$ & $1.6$ & $1$ & $0.6$ \\
$0.6\times2$ & $1.2$ & $1$ & $0.2$ \quad$\leftarrow$ seen at row 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The carry has returned to a value it already took, so from here the
work repeats forever, the block $0011$ recurring without end:
\begin{equation*}
  0.1_{10} \;=\; 0.0\overline{0011}_{2}
            \;=\; 0.000110011001100\ldots_{2} .
  \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{equation*}
\end{ex}

\noindent This is the phenomenon flagged at the end of \S3.4, and the rule
behind it is worth stating. A fraction $p/q$ in lowest terms terminates in base
$b$ exactly when every prime factor of $q$ divides $b$. Base $10$ has prime
factors $2$ and $5$; base $2$ has only $2$. So $1/10$ terminates in decimal and
cannot terminate in binary, because of the factor of $5$ that base $2$ has no
way to absorb.

\begin{warn}[$0.1+0.2\neq0.3$]
Type \texttt{0.1 + 0.2} into Python, or JavaScript, or C, and you get
\texttt{0.30000000000000004}. This is not a bug in the language, and every
language does it. Neither $0.1$ nor $0.2$ is a floating-point number ---
by the example above, neither has a finite binary expansion --- so the machine
stores the nearest float to each. It then adds those two stored values exactly
and rounds again. The rounding of a sum need not equal the sum of the
roundings, and here it does not. The consequence for you is a habit: never test
two computed floating-point quantities for equality with \texttt{==}. Test
whether they differ by less than a tolerance you have chosen on purpose.
\end{warn}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Normalized form}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A number has many representations as a digit string times a power of two:
\[
  1101.1011_{2}
  \;=\; 1101.1011_{2}\times2^{0}
  \;=\; 110.11011_{2}\times2^{1}
  \;=\; 1.1011011_{2}\times2^{3}
  \;=\; 0.11011011_{2}\times2^{4},
\]
all equal, all shifting the point against a matching change of exponent. Storing
a number in hardware requires picking one of them, once and for all.

\begin{defn}[Normalized form]
A nonzero binary number is in \textbf{normalized form} when it is written
\[
  \pm\, 1.f_1f_2f_3\cdots \times 2^{e},
\]
that is, with exactly one nonzero digit to the left of the point. The full
quantity $1.f_1f_2\cdots$ is the \textbf{significand}; the digits
$f_1f_2\cdots$ after the point are the \textbf{mantissa}, or fraction; and $e$
is the \textbf{exponent}.
\end{defn}

\noindent To normalize, shift the point until it sits immediately after the
leading $1$. The exponent is the number of places shifted: positive if you moved
left, negative if you moved right.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{As given} & \textbf{Shift} & \textbf{Normalized} \\
\midrule
$1101.1011_{2}$ & $3$ places left  & $1.1011011_{2}\times2^{3}$ \\
$0.0001101_{2}$ & $4$ places right & $1.101_{2}\times2^{-4}$ \\
$1_{2}$         & none             & $1.0_{2}\times2^{0}$ \\
$10.0_{2}$      & $1$ place left   & $1.0_{2}\times2^{1}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Now look at what every entry in the right-hand column has in common.
The digit before the point is $1$. It is $1$ in every row, and it must be: base
$2$ has exactly one nonzero digit, and normalized form demands that the leading
digit be nonzero. A quantity that is the same in every case carries no
information, and there is no reason to spend storage on it. That observation is
worth a full bit, and \S3.8 collects it.

\begin{rmk}
Zero is the exception, and it has to be. It has no nonzero leading digit, so it
has no normalized form at all, and no choice of $e$ produces it. It is handled
by a reserved bit pattern instead --- \S3.10.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A standard: IEEE 754}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Normalized form tells us \emph{what} to store: a sign, a mantissa and an
exponent. It does not say how many bits each gets, in what order, how to round
what will not fit, or what to do when a result is too large to represent. Before
the mid-1980s each manufacturer answered those questions its own way. The same
program, compiled on two machines, could return two different numbers, and there
was no way to say which was correct.

The \textbf{Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers} --- the IEEE,
pronounced ``eye-triple-E'' --- ended that with the IEEE 754 standard, first
published in 1985 and revised since. It fixes the formats, the rounding rules
and the behaviour on overflow and on invalid operations. Essentially every
processor and every language you will use implements it, so ``floating point''
in practice means IEEE 754. The rest of this section is a description of that
standard.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{IEEE name} & \textbf{Common name} & \textbf{Width} & \textbf{Typical use} \\
\midrule
binary16  & half precision   & $16$ bits  & machine learning, graphics \\
binary32  & single precision, \texttt{float}  & $32$ bits  & graphics, embedded, ML \\
binary64  & double precision, \texttt{double} & $64$ bits  & the default nearly everywhere \\
binary128 & quadruple precision & $128$ bits & specialised high-accuracy work \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent We will work with binary16 and binary32, because they are short enough
to write out in full on the page. Everything said about them holds verbatim for
binary64 with three numbers changed.

\subsection{The three fields, and the hidden 1}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A floating-point number is one bit string, cut into three consecutive fields.

  \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt,mirror},gray!75]

  \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt,mirror},gray!75]

  \node[below,align=center,font=\scriptsize,ForestGreen] at (11,-0.6)
       {mantissa $f$\\$10$ bits};
  \node[above,font=\scriptsize,gray] at (8,1.15)
       {binary16, holding $13.6875$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent Write $s$ for the sign bit, $E$ for the exponent field read as an
unsigned integer, and $f$ for the mantissa read as the digits after a binary
point. For a \emph{normal} number the value stored is
\[
  \boxed{\;x \;=\; (-1)^{s}\times 1.f \times 2^{\,E-\text{bias}}\;}
\]
with the bias a fixed constant of the format, discussed in \S3.9.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Format} & \textbf{Total} & \textbf{Sign} & \textbf{Exponent $k$}
                & \textbf{Mantissa $m$} & \textbf{Bias} \\
\midrule
binary16 & $16$ & $1$ & $5$  & $10$ & $15$ \\
binary32 & $32$ & $1$ & $8$  & $23$ & $127$ \\
binary64 & $64$ & $1$ & $11$ & $52$ & $1023$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{The hidden 1}
Notice what the formula does and the bit string does not. The value is built
from $1.f$, but only $f$ is stored. The leading $1$ is never written down
anywhere in the sixteen bits --- the hardware supplies it when decoding. It is
called the \textbf{hidden bit}, or the implicit leading bit, and \S3.6 is the
reason it can be omitted: in normalized binary the leading digit is always $1$,
so writing it would be storing a constant.

\begin{defn}[Precision]
The \textbf{precision} $p$ of a binary floating-point format is the number of
significand digits, counting the hidden bit. If the mantissa field holds $m$
bits then
\[
  p = m+1 .
\]
\end{defn}

\noindent So binary16 has $p=11$ from a $10$-bit field, binary32 has $p=24$ from
$23$ bits, and binary64 has $p=53$ from $52$. The hidden bit is a free doubling
of the number of representable values, at no storage cost --- and it is
available only because we are in base $2$. In base $10$ the leading digit could
be any of $1$ through $9$, and would have to be written down.

\begin{ex}[Encoding]
Store $13.6875$ in binary16.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \emph{Sign.} The number is positive, so $s=0$.
  \item \emph{Convert.} By \S3.5, $13.6875 = 1101.1011_{2}$.
  \item \emph{Normalize.} By \S3.6, $1101.1011_{2}=1.1011011_{2}\times2^{3}$, so
        $e=3$ and the mantissa digits are $1011011$.
  \item \emph{Bias the exponent.} $E = e+15 = 18 = 10010_{2}$, five bits.
  \item \emph{Pad the mantissa} on the right to ten bits:
        $1011011\underline{000}$.
\end{enumerate}
The stored string is
\[
  \underbrace{\texttt{0}}_{s}\;
  \underbrace{\texttt{10010}}_{E=18}\;
  \underbrace{\texttt{1011011000}}_{f},
\]
which is the string in the diagram above. In binary32 the same number becomes
\[
  \underbrace{\texttt{0}}_{s}\;
  \underbrace{\texttt{10000010}}_{E=130}\;
  \underbrace{\texttt{10110110000000000000000}}_{f},
\]
the exponent field now holding $3+127=130$ and the mantissa padded to
$23$ bits. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\begin{ex}[Decoding]
What binary16 number is $\texttt{1}\;\texttt{10001}\;\texttt{0100000000}$?

The sign bit is $1$, so the number is negative. The exponent field reads
$10001_{2}=17$, and unbiasing gives $e=17-15=2$. Restoring the hidden bit, the
significand is $1.01_{2}=1.25$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
  x = (-1)^{1}\times 1.25\times 2^{2} = -5 .
  \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{equation*}
\end{ex}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The bias, and the range of the exponent}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Exponents must be allowed to be negative --- that is what represents numbers
smaller than $1$. There are two ways to store a signed integer in a fixed field,
and IEEE 754 chose the simpler one: store not $e$ but $e+\text{bias}$, an
unsigned integer, where for a $k$-bit exponent field
\[
  \text{bias} = 2^{\,k-1}-1 .
\]
For $k=5$ that is $15$, for $k=8$ it is $127$, for $k=11$ it is $1023$ --- the
constants tabulated in \S3.8. Decoding just subtracts: $e = E-\text{bias}$.

Two exponent fields are not available for this, because they are reserved to
mark the special values of \S3.10: the all-zeros field $E=0$ and the all-ones
field $E=2^{k}-1$. So a normal number has $E$ somewhere in
$1\le E\le 2^{k}-2$, and subtracting the bias gives the range of usable
exponents.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Format} & \textbf{Stored $E$} & \textbf{Exponent $e=E-\text{bias}$}
                & \textbf{Range of $e$} \\
\midrule
binary16 & $1\ldots30$   & $E-15$   & $-14 \le e \le 15$ \\
binary32 & $1\ldots254$  & $E-127$  & $-126 \le e \le 127$ \\
binary64 & $1\ldots2046$ & $E-1023$ & $-1022 \le e \le 1023$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{rmk}
Why bias rather than a sign bit on the exponent? Because biasing makes the
exponent fields sort in the same order as the exponents they denote. A
consequence is that two positive floats can be compared simply by comparing
their bit strings as unsigned integers --- the hardware needs no special
floating-point comparison circuit at all. That the sign bit comes first, the
exponent second and the mantissa last is part of the same design: it puts the
fields in decreasing order of significance.
\end{rmk}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The reserved patterns: zero, infinity and NaN}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The two reserved exponent fields, combined with whether the mantissa is zero,
give four cases.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Exponent field $E$} & \textbf{Mantissa $f$} & \textbf{Meaning}
  & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
all zeros & $f=0$    & signed zero  & $\pm 0$ \\
all zeros & $f\neq0$ & subnormal    & $(-1)^{s}\,0.f\times2^{\,1-\text{bias}}$ \\
$1\ldots2^{k}-2$ & any & normal     & $(-1)^{s}\,1.f\times2^{\,E-\text{bias}}$ \\
all ones  & $f=0$    & infinity     & $\pm\infty$ \\
all ones  & $f\neq0$ & not a number & \texttt{NaN} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent In binary16, for instance,
\[
  \texttt{0}\;\texttt{11111}\;\texttt{0000000000} = +\infty,
  \qquad
  \texttt{1}\;\texttt{11111}\;\texttt{0000000000} = -\infty,
  \qquad
  \texttt{0}\;\texttt{11111}\;\texttt{1000000000} = \texttt{NaN} .
\]

\subsubsection*{Infinity: $\pm\infty$}
Infinity is produced by \emph{overflow} --- a result whose magnitude exceeds the
largest representable number --- and by division of a nonzero number by zero. It
is a value, not a crash: the computation continues, with $\infty+1=\infty$ and
$1/\infty = 0$. Two infinities are needed because the two directions of overflow
must be distinguished.

\subsubsection*{Subnormals}
The pattern $E=0$ with $f\neq0$ drops the hidden bit and reads the significand as
$0.f$, which fills in the gap between zero and the smallest normal number with a
few evenly spaced values. Without them there would be a sudden cliff at the
bottom of the range, and two distinct small numbers could have a difference that
rounds to zero even though they are not equal.

\subsubsection*{Not a number: \texttt{NaN}}
\texttt{NaN} stands for \emph{not a number}, and it is what an operation returns
when no numerical answer is defensible: $0/0$, $\infty-\infty$, $\infty/\infty$,
$\sqrt{-1}$. Two properties make it worth knowing about.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \texttt{NaN} \emph{propagates}. Any arithmetic operation with a
        \texttt{NaN} input returns \texttt{NaN}. One of them entering a long
        computation contaminates everything downstream of it.
  \item \texttt{NaN} is not equal to itself. The comparison
        $\texttt{NaN}=\texttt{NaN}$ is false --- it is the only value in the
        system for which $x=x$ fails.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warn}[A vector of \texttt{NaN}s]
The second property is deliberate, and it is how you test for one: the idiom
\texttt{x != x} is true exactly when \texttt{x} is \texttt{NaN}. The first
property is why, when a numerical program goes wrong, the symptom is so often an
output vector every entry of which is \texttt{NaN}. The final state tells you
nothing about the cause; a single division by zero thousands of operations
earlier will produce it. Finding where the contamination started, rather than
where it surfaced, is the work.
\end{warn}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The range of a floating-point system}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
With the exponent range from \S3.9 we can say exactly how large and how small
the members of $\F$ are. The largest magnitude pairs the largest exponent
$e_{\max}=\text{bias}$ with the largest significand, which is
$1.11\cdots1_{2}$ with $p-1$ ones, namely $2-2^{\,1-p}$. The smallest positive
normal magnitude pairs the smallest exponent $e_{\min}=1-\text{bias}$ with the
smallest significand, $1.00\cdots0_{2}=1$. So
\[
  x_{\max} = \bigl(2-2^{\,1-p}\bigr)\,2^{\,e_{\max}},
  \qquad
  x_{\min} = 2^{\,e_{\min}} .
\]

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Format} & \textbf{Smallest positive normal} & \textbf{Largest}
  & \textbf{Precision $p$} \\
\midrule
binary16 & $2^{-14}\approx6.10\times10^{-5}$    & $65504$
         & $11$ \\
binary32 & $2^{-126}\approx1.18\times10^{-38}$  & $\approx3.40\times10^{38}$
         & $24$ \\
binary64 & $2^{-1022}\approx2.23\times10^{-308}$& $\approx1.80\times10^{308}$
         & $53$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Anything larger in magnitude than $x_{\max}$ overflows to $\pm\infty$;
anything smaller than the smallest subnormal underflows to $\pm0$. And note
which field controls which: the \emph{exponent} bits buy range, the
\emph{mantissa} bits buy precision, and they are two independent budgets. Going
from binary16 to binary32 spends $3$ more bits on range and $13$ more on
precision.

\begin{rmk}
Binary16's ceiling of $65504$ is low enough to hit by accident, which is exactly
what happens when neural networks are trained in half precision for speed:
intermediate quantities overflow to $\infty$, the $\infty$ turns into a
\texttt{NaN} at the next subtraction, and training collapses. The standard
remedy --- multiplying through by a constant to move the computation into range,
then dividing it out afterwards --- is called loss scaling, and it is a working
engineer's response to the table above.
\end{rmk}

\lookahead We now know precisely which numbers a machine can hold. The
remaining question is the one that matters for computation: what happens to
everything else. How far apart are the dots, how much is lost when a real number
is pushed to the nearest one, and what does that do to an algorithm that
performs the operation five thousand times?

\section{Precision, spacing and error}
%==============================================================================
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Machine precision}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Start at $1$ and ask for the next floating-point number. In normalized form
$1 = 1.00\cdots0_{2}\times2^{0}$, and the smallest change we can make is to set
the last mantissa bit, giving $1.00\cdots01_{2}\times2^{0}=1+2^{-(p-1)}$. There
is nothing in between, because there is no bit left to change.

\begin{defn}[Machine precision]
The \textbf{machine precision}, or machine epsilon, of a binary floating-point
system of precision $p$ is
\[
  \epsm = 2^{\,1-p},
\]
the distance from $1$ to the next larger floating-point number.
\end{defn}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Format} & \textbf{$p$} & \textbf{$\epsm=2^{1-p}$}
  & \textbf{Decimal digits} \\
\midrule
binary16 & $11$ & $2^{-10}\approx9.77\times10^{-4}$  & about $3$ \\
binary32 & $24$ & $2^{-23}\approx1.19\times10^{-7}$  & about $7$ \\
binary64 & $53$ & $2^{-52}\approx2.22\times10^{-16}$ & about $16$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The last column is where the informal claim of \S3.3 came from. A
\texttt{float} was said to carry ``about $7$ decimal digits'' and a
\texttt{double} ``about $16$''; those are just $\epsm$
written in scientific notation and the exponent read off.

\begin{warn}[$\epsm$ is not the smallest positive number]
These are two different quantities answering two different questions, and they
differ by more than thirty orders of magnitude. In binary32 the smallest
positive normal number is about $1.18\times10^{-38}$, while
$\epsm$ is about $1.19\times10^{-7}$. The first asks how
small a magnitude the format can reach, and is set by the exponent field. The
second asks how finely the format separates numbers near $1$, and is set by the
mantissa field. A number far smaller than $\epsm$ is
perfectly representable; what you cannot do is \emph{add} it to $1$ and see any
change.
\end{warn}

\noindent One companion constant, needed in \S4.5. Half of the machine precision
is called the \textbf{unit roundoff},
\[
  u = \tfrac12\epsm = 2^{-p},
\]
and it will turn out to be the largest relative error that rounding can commit.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Units in the last place}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Machine precision measures the spacing near $1$. Everywhere else the spacing is
different, and \S3.2 already showed the pattern in a picture. Here is the
statement behind it.

Fix an exponent $e$ and consider all the numbers with that exponent: they are
$1.f\times2^{e}$ with $f$ running over every one of the $(p-1)$-bit mantissa
patterns. Consecutive ones differ by one unit in the last mantissa bit, which is
$2^{-(p-1)}$ in the significand, hence $2^{-(p-1)}\times2^{e}$ in value.

\begin{defn}[Unit in the last place]
Let $x$ satisfy $2^{e}\le|x|<2^{e+1}$. The \textbf{unit in the last place} at
$x$ is the distance between consecutive floating-point numbers there:
\[
  \operatorname{ulp}(x) = 2^{\,e-(p-1)} = 2^{e}\,\epsm .
\]
\end{defn}

\noindent Read the formula twice, once for each factor.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=4pt]
  \item The $2^{e}$ says the \emph{absolute} spacing scales with the magnitude:
        double the number and you double the gap to its neighbour. This is the
        doubling visible in the figure of \S3.2.
  \item The $\epsm$ says the \emph{relative} spacing does
        not change. Dividing through, $\operatorname{ulp}(x)/|x|$ lies between
        $\epsm/2$ and $\epsm$ wherever
        $x$ sits. The system carries the same number of significant digits at
        every scale.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The second point is what the name \emph{floating} point refers to. The
binary point is not nailed to a fixed column, as it is in a fixed-point system
where all numbers are equally spaced; it floats to wherever the leading digit
is, so that the available digits are always spent on the part of the number that
matters.

\subsubsection*{A toy system, $p=2$}
Take base $2$, precision $p=2$ --- one hidden bit and a single mantissa bit ---
and exponents $-1\le e\le2$. The significand is either $1.0_{2}=1$ or
$1.1_{2}=1.5$, and that is all. Every positive number in this $\F$:

  \draw[gray!60,{Stealth[length=1.6mm]}-{Stealth[length=1.6mm]}]
        (4,-0.45) -- (6,-0.45);
  \node[font=\scriptsize,gray] at (5,-0.82) {$2$};
  \node[left,font=\scriptsize,gray] at (-0.2,-0.45) {$\operatorname{ulp}$:};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent The open circle at $8$ is not a member of the toy system: it would
need $e=3$, and the exponent stops at $2$. Check the definition against the
picture. Between $1$ and $2$ the
spacing is $\operatorname{ulp}=2^{0-1}=0.5$; between $2$ and $4$ it is
$2^{1-1}=1$; between $4$ and $8$ it is $2^{2-1}=2$. And the machine precision of
this system is $\epsm=2^{1-2}=0.5$, which is exactly the gap
from $1$ to the next number, $1.5$. Small as it is, this system has every
feature of binary64; it merely has $8$ positive numbers instead of about
$10^{308}$.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Rounding to nearest}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A real number that is not a dot must be moved to one. Which one is a policy
decision, and IEEE 754 offers several \emph{rounding modes}. One is the default
everywhere, and it is the only one we will use.

\begin{defn}[Round to nearest]
The map $\operatorname{round}:\R\to\F$ sends $x$ to the floating-point number
closest to it. When $x$ falls exactly halfway between two neighbours, the tie is
broken by choosing the one whose last mantissa bit is $0$ --- ``round half to
even''.
\end{defn}

\begin{ex}
In the toy system of \S4.2:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item $\operatorname{round}(2.6)=3$. The neighbours are $2$ and $3$, at
        distances $0.6$ and $0.4$.
  \item $\operatorname{round}(1.2)=1$. The neighbours are $1$ and $1.5$, at
        distances $0.2$ and $0.3$.
  \item $\operatorname{round}(2.5)=2$. The neighbours $2$ and $3$ are both at
        distance $0.5$, so this is a tie. Now $2=1.0_{2}\times2^{1}$ has last
        mantissa bit $0$ and $3=1.1_{2}\times2^{1}$ has last mantissa bit $1$,
        so the rule selects $2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{ex}

\begin{rmk}
The tie rule looks fussy and is not. Always rounding halves upward would push
every tie in the same direction, and in a sum of a million terms those pushes
accumulate into a systematic drift. Sending half the ties down and half up lets
them cancel instead.
\end{rmk}

\noindent The bound that matters follows immediately from \S4.2. If $x$ lies in
the normalized range, it sits between two neighbours a distance
$\operatorname{ulp}(x)$ apart, and the nearer of the two can be no further away
than half of that:
\[
  \boxed{\;\bigl|\operatorname{round}(x)-x\bigr|
        \;\le\; \tfrac12\operatorname{ulp}(x) = 2^{\,e-p}\;}
\]
whenever $2^{e}\le|x|<2^{e+1}$. Everything in \S4.5 is a consequence of that one
line. Outside the normalized range the bound does not apply: above $x_{\max}$
rounding overflows to $\pm\infty$, and below the subnormal floor it underflows
to $\pm0$.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Absolute and relative error}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We need vocabulary for how wrong an approximation is. Two measures, and they
answer different questions.

\begin{defn}[Absolute and relative error]
Let $\hat{x}$ approximate a real number $x$. The \textbf{absolute error} is
\[
  E_{\text{abs}} = \lvert \hat{x}-x\rvert ,
\]
and, for $x\neq0$, the \textbf{relative error} is
\[
  E_{\text{rel}} = \frac{\lvert \hat{x}-x\rvert}{\lvert x\rvert} .
\]
\end{defn}

\begin{ex}
In the toy system, $\operatorname{round}(2.6)=3$. So
\[
  E_{\text{abs}} = |3-2.6| = 0.4,
  \qquad
  E_{\text{rel}} = \frac{0.4}{2.6} \approx 0.1538 .
\]
Compare that relative error with the unit roundoff of this system,
$u = 2^{-p}=2^{-2}=0.25$. As promised, $0.1538 \le 0.25$. \hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\noindent Relative error is the one that travels. An absolute error of $0.4$ is
catastrophic in a quantity of size $1$ and beneath notice in a quantity of size
$10^{6}$; the number alone does not say which situation you are in. Relative
error is scale-free, and --- by the second reading of $\operatorname{ulp}$ in
\S4.2 --- it is precisely the quantity a floating-point system holds under
control. The absolute spacing of $\F$ varies over three hundred orders of
magnitude; the relative spacing never leaves a factor of two.

\subsubsection*{The same definitions for vectors}
Nothing changes except the measuring stick. Where a scalar used absolute value,
a vector uses a norm --- the Euclidean norm of the vector unit, \S2, will do.

\begin{defn}[Error in a vector]
Let $\hat{\vc{v}}$ approximate $\vc{v}\in\R^{n}$. Then
\[
  E_{\text{abs}} = \norm{\hat{\vc{v}}-\vc{v}},
  \qquad
  E_{\text{rel}} = \frac{\norm{\hat{\vc{v}}-\vc{v}}}{\norm{\vc{v}}}
  \quad (\vc{v}\neq\vc{0}) .
\]
\end{defn}

\noindent Both are single numbers describing the whole vector, not one number
per entry. What they measure is the length of the \emph{error vector}
$\hat{\vc{v}}-\vc{v}$, which is exactly the picture the vector unit built:
$\norm{\hat{\vc{v}}-\vc{v}}$ is the distance between the two points.

\begin{ex}
Take $\vc{v}=(1,\,2.6)$ and round each entry into the toy system, giving
$\hat{\vc{v}}=(1,\,3)$. The error vector is $\hat{\vc{v}}-\vc{v}=(0,\,0.4)$, so
\[
  E_{\text{abs}} = \norm{(0,0.4)} = 0.4,
  \qquad
  E_{\text{rel}} = \frac{0.4}{\norm{(1,2.6)}}
                 = \frac{0.4}{\sqrt{7.76}} \approx 0.1436 .
\]
Note that the first entry was stored exactly and contributed nothing; the
relative error of the vector is not the relative error of its worst entry.
\hfill$\blacksquare$
\end{ex}

\subsection{Floating-point arithmetic}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here is the difficulty the whole section has been building towards. Let
$x,y\in\F$. Their exact sum $x+y$ is a real number, and there is no reason
whatever for it to lie in $\F$ --- in the toy system, $1.5$ and $2$ are both
members and $1.5+2=3.5$ is not. So the machine \emph{cannot} return $x+y$. What
it returns instead is fixed by the standard.

\begin{defn}[Floating-point arithmetic]
For $x,y\in\F$ and an operation $\ast\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}$, the corresponding
machine operation is
\[
  \operatorname{fl}(x\ast y) = \operatorname{round}(x\ast y) :
\]
form the exact result, then round it once to the nearest floating-point number.
IEEE 754 requires this of all four arithmetic operations and of the square root.
\end{defn}

\noindent This is a strong guarantee, and it is worth being clear about what it
does and does not promise. It does not say the answer is correct. It says the
answer is the \emph{best possible} --- the closest member of $\F$ to the true
result --- and that each operation commits at most one rounding. A machine that
did anything less would be much harder to reason about.

Now we can quantify the damage.

\begin{thm}[Rounding as a relative perturbation]
Let $x$ be any real number in the normalized range of a binary floating-point
system with precision $p$. Then
\[
  \operatorname{round}(x) = x(1+\delta)
  \qquad\text{for some } \delta \text{ with }
  |\delta| < \epsm .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
If $x=0$ then $\operatorname{round}(x)=0$ and $\delta=0$ works, so assume
$x\neq0$ and let $e$ be the exponent with $2^{e}\le|x|<2^{e+1}$. Define
\[
  \delta = \frac{\operatorname{round}(x)-x}{x},
\]
which is legitimate since $x\neq0$, and rearranges at once to
$\operatorname{round}(x)=x(1+\delta)$. It remains to bound $\delta$. By the
rounding bound of \S4.3 the numerator satisfies
$|\operatorname{round}(x)-x|\le\tfrac12\operatorname{ulp}(x)=2^{\,e-p}$, and the
denominator satisfies $|x|\ge2^{e}$. Therefore
\[
  |\delta| = \frac{|\operatorname{round}(x)-x|}{|x|}
           \;\le\; \frac{2^{\,e-p}}{2^{e}}
           \;=\; 2^{-p}
           \;=\; \tfrac12\epsm
           \;<\; \epsm . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{rmk}
The proof delivers slightly more than the statement: it gives
$|\delta|\le 2^{-p}=u$, the unit roundoff of \S4.1, which is half of
$\epsm$. Both forms are in common use, and the sharper one
is the standard hypothesis in numerical analysis. Nothing in what follows
depends on which you carry.
\end{rmk}

\noindent Combining the theorem with the definition of $\operatorname{fl}$ gives
the statement that every error analysis in numerical linear algebra starts from:
for $x,y\in\F$ and $\ast\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}$,
\[
  \boxed{\;\operatorname{fl}(x\ast y) = (x\ast y)(1+\delta),
          \qquad |\delta|\le u .\;}
\]
Read it in words: \emph{every floating-point operation returns the exact answer,
perturbed by a relative amount no larger than the unit roundoff}. One operation
is harmless --- in binary64 the perturbation is at the sixteenth digit. The
subject begins when you chain $N$ of them together, because those $N$
perturbations may cancel or may compound, and which one happens is a property of
the algorithm rather than of the hardware. An algorithm whose errors stay
controlled is called \emph{stable}, and one whose errors amplify is not; two
mathematically identical formulas, like the two versions of the projection in
\S1.6, can differ in this respect as well as in cost.

\begin{warn}[Addition is not associative]
In $\R$, $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$ always. In $\F$ it can fail. Work in binary16, where
$\epsm=2^{-10}$, and take $a=1$, $b=c=2^{-11}$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{Left to right.} The exact sum $1+2^{-11}$ falls exactly halfway
        between $1$ and $1+2^{-10}$, its two neighbours. Round half to even
        selects $1$. Adding $c$ to that repeats the same tie, so
        $\operatorname{fl}\bigl(\operatorname{fl}(a+b)+c\bigr) = 1$.
  \item \emph{Right to left.} First $2^{-11}+2^{-11}=2^{-10}$, which is exactly
        representable, so no rounding occurs. Then $1+2^{-10}$ is exactly
        representable too, giving
        $\operatorname{fl}\bigl(a+\operatorname{fl}(b+c)\bigr) = 1+2^{-10}$.
\end{itemize}
The two orders disagree. The practical consequences are real: summing a long
list of numbers gives different answers depending on the order, running the same
sum in parallel gives a different answer again because the partial sums combine
differently, and a compiler that reorders arithmetic to go faster changes your
output. None of this is a defect in the hardware. It is what working in a finite
set costs.
\end{warn}

\subsection{FLOPs}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We can now close the loop back to \S1. That section defined an \emph{operation}
as one addition, subtraction, multiplication, division or square root, and
counted them on vectors in $\R^{n}$ --- but $\R^{n}$ was a fiction, as this
section has spent its length establishing. The operations were on $\F$ all
along, and the standard name says so.

\begin{defn}[FLOP]
A \textbf{floating-point operation}, or \textbf{FLOP}, is a single arithmetic
operation --- one add, subtract, multiply, divide or square root --- performed
on floating-point numbers.
\end{defn}

\noindent Nothing about the counting changes; only the name, which is now
accurate. Restating the results of \S1 in the standard vocabulary: for
$\vc{u},\vc{v}\in\F^{n}$,

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Computation} & \textbf{Cost} & \textbf{Complexity} \\
\midrule
$\vc{u}+\vc{v}$                    & $n$ FLOPs    & $O(n)$ \\
$\langle\vc{u},\vc{v}\rangle$      & $2n-1$ FLOPs & $O(n)$ \\
$\norm{\vc{u}}$                    & $2n$ FLOPs   & $O(n)$ \\
$\proj{\vc{u}}{\vc{v}}$            & $5n-1$ FLOPs & $O(n)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent And there is a second reading of the table now available. Each of
those FLOPs, by \S4.5, perturbs its result by a relative amount up to $u$. A dot
product in $\R^{1000}$ performs $1999$ of them, so it commits $1999$ roundings
on the way to a single number. Its cost and its accuracy are both consequences
of the same count.

\begin{warn}[FLOPs and FLOPS are different things]
The two are distinguished, unhelpfully, by the case of the final letter.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{FLOPs} --- lower-case \emph{s}, a plural --- counts operations.
        It is a property of an algorithm, and it is what \S1 computed.
  \item \textbf{FLOPS} --- upper-case \emph{S}, for \emph{per second} --- is a
        rate. It is a property of hardware. A processor rated at $30$
        teraFLOPS performs up to $3\times10^{13}$ floating-point operations
        every second.
\end{itemize}
Dividing one by the other estimates a running time,
\[
  \text{time} \;\approx\; \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{FLOPS}} ,
\]
which is optimistic --- it assumes the processor never waits for data to arrive
from memory, and in practice it often does --- but it is the right first
calculation, and it is why a FLOP count is worth having as a number and not only
as a complexity class.
\end{warn}

\noindent FLOP counts are the standard currency of scientific computing. The
cost of solving a linear system, of factoring a matrix, of training a neural
network, is quoted in FLOPs, and the capability of a machine is quoted in FLOPS.
Both halves of this lecture were needed to make sense of either.

\lookahead Three tools have been assembled here: an exact operation count
(\S1), the growth rate that count belongs to (\S2), and the arithmetic the count
is actually performed in (\S3--\S4). From this point on, every algorithm we meet
gets reported the same way --- what it costs in FLOPs, what its complexity is,
and what rounding does to its answer. The first of them will operate not on
vectors but on matrices, and its cost will not be $O(n)$.
